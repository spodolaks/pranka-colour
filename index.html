§01

One look across a whole set

This is the part that is worth paying for. A single frame graded well is a nice picture; forty frames graded identically is a deliverable - and it is where most cheap editing falls apart, because running one preset over a take is not the same thing as matching it. Three projects below. Each opens with an uncropped frame on a slider, so the grade is the only thing that changes, then frames where the recrop is part of the work, then the contact sheet.

Case one

Narrative series: flat daylight in, one night out

A ten-frame story shot at midday and delivered as a single nocturnal scene. The look is built once on a reference frame, then argued with image by image - green pulled almost out, exposure taken down, every figure dodged back up by hand so nobody disappears into the grade.

The reference frame the rest of the set was built from, and the clearest look at what the grade does on its own: summer green pulled almost entirely out, the whole scene taken down to dusk, and the figure and the paper held up so they survive it.

Case three

Conceptual fashion series: nineteen frames, one grade

The hardest of the three. White paper masks and white swimwear in full mountain sun, so almost every frame came off the card with the highlights clipped: the recovery had to happen before any look could be applied. Then a crop per frame to carry the story, and a selective preset built for each mask so the paper stays paper-white while everything around it drops two or three stops. Doing that once is an edit. Doing it nineteen times and still having one series is the work. Delivered as full-resolution masters.

§03

Colour casts and mixed light

The most common thing a full take comes back with: a tint bias over every single raw, or two light sources that refuse to agree. On one frame it is two minutes of work. Across a hundred it is the whole job, because the correction has to be the same correction or the set stops looking like one set. a* below is the green-magenta axis, which is what a cast actually is.

Before After

Rocks, riding coat

The strongest magenta cast in this part of the take and the easiest to see it go: the stone comes back grey, the white trousers come back white, and the contrast lift is what gives the rock its texture again.

Measured

Portraits on location

Fringing on the water

Specular highlights on moving water are where a fast lens shows its aberration: every bright edge carries a magenta and green split. No amount of global saturation work fixes it, because it sits on edges rather than on a colour, and a grade applied over the top only makes it louder. Taken out first. The green that remains is the look, put there afterwards on purpose.

Crop
  • 340 px of the frame
  • a* > +10: 10.4% → 0.2%

The number printed on the mask

The masks were folded from printed sheets, and the sheets carry the maker's marks. On a face this size nobody sees it on a screen; at any print size it is the first thing that gives the prop away. Off, without flattening the fold it sits on.

How a job runs

STEP 01

Send three frames

Not the whole shoot - three files that represent it. I grade one of them free and send it back. If the look is wrong, it is wrong after one frame instead of after a hundred.

STEP 02

Agree the look, then the set

Once the reference frame is signed off, everything else is matched to it - not by running one preset over the take, but frame by frame until nothing drifts.

STEP 03

Changes, then delivery

Marked up per frame or across the set: warmer, less contrast in the shadows, crop this one wider. Two rounds included, delivered in the format and colour space you publish in.

The part worth paying for is the hundredth frame

Anyone can make one picture look good. The work is making a hundred look like they came from the same afternoon, and doing it on a deadline.

Fifteen years of never missing one - most recently running CRM operations, where the delivery either goes out on schedule or it does not go out at all. That habit is as much of the offer as the grading is.
